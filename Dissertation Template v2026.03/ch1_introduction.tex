\section{Introduction} 
\label{sec:Introduction} 
This chapter introduces a study that examined \emph{the role of \gls{it} leadership in realising the \gls{roi} of \gls{ai} coding assistants}, as perceived by software engineering practitioners in \gls{sa}. The chapter begins with setting the background for the research and the research problem that the study sought to address. Thereafter, the research objective, aim, and the research questions are set out, and how each research question was addressed through the hypotheses or analysis is shown. The chapter thereafter discusses the importance, scope, limitations, and delimitations of the study, as well as the theoretical framework on which the research was based. The chapter ends with a brief discussion of the structure of the dissertation.

\section{Background} 
\label{sec:Background} 

\gls{ai} has been described as a general-purpose technology whose productivity benefits depend on substantial complementary investment in organisational processes, skills and management practices \parencite{Brynjolfsson2021, Babina2024}. Since the public release of large language model tools in late 2022, \gls{genai} has moved from experimentation towards routine use in knowledge work \parencite{Gillespie2025, Hughes2026}. Organisations have invested in these tools in the expectation of efficiency gains and improved process performance, yet surveys of managers indicate that only a minority of organisations report significant financial benefits from their \gls{ai} initiatives \parencite{Ransbotham2020}. \gls{is} research has accordingly shifted its emphasis from whether organisations adopt \gls{ai} to how, and under which organisational conditions, \gls{ai} investments create business value \parencite{Enholm2022, Berente2021, Mikalef2021}.

In software engineering, \gls{ai} coding assistants are among the most visible applications of \gls{genai}. These tools are embedded in the development environment and provide context-sensitive code completion, generate whole functions, explain and document code, and support debugging \parencite{LiangJT2024, Sergeyuk2025}. Experimental and field studies report that these tools can accelerate programming work. In a controlled experiment, developers with access to GitHub Copilot completed a defined programming task 55.8\% faster than a control group \parencite{Peng2023}. Field experiments at three large companies found that access to an \gls{ai} coding assistant increased the number of completed tasks by approximately 26\% on average \parencite{Cui2026}, and analyses of telemetry from a large user population linked the acceptance of \gls{ai} suggestions to developers' perceived productivity \parencite{Ziegler2024}. Comparable gains have been reported for other forms of knowledge work \parencite{Noy2023, Brynjolfsson2025}. Adoption has since become widespread: the 2025 DORA research programme reported that 90\% of the software development professionals it surveyed used \gls{ai} \parencite{DORA2025}, and 84\% of respondents to the 2025 Stack Overflow Developer Survey used or planned to use \gls{ai} tools in their development process \parencite{StackOverflow2025}.

Productivity gains demonstrated in controlled settings, however, do not necessarily translate into sustained organisational performance. Economic research shows that the gains from \gls{ai} often fail to appear in organisational performance measures until complementary changes in processes, skills and capabilities have been made \parencite{Brynjolfsson2021}. In a 2025 global survey, 88\% of respondents reported regular use of \gls{ai} in at least one business function, but only 39\% reported any impact of \gls{ai} on earnings at the enterprise level \parencite{McKinsey2025}. Firm-level studies likewise indicate that the returns from \gls{ai} investment are heterogeneous and depend on complementary organisational capabilities \parencite{Babina2024, Mikalef2021}. Industry evidence from software delivery points in the same direction: the 2024 DORA research programme found that higher \gls{ai} adoption was associated with improvements in documentation quality and code review speed, but also with small estimated decreases in software delivery throughput and stability \parencite{Harvey2024}.

The evidence on productivity gains is also far from uniform. In the field experiments reported by \textcite{Cui2026}, less experienced developers benefited considerably more than senior developers. A randomised controlled trial with experienced open-source developers working on mature code bases found that access to early-2025 \gls{ai} tools increased task completion time by 19\%, even though the developers themselves expected to be faster \parencite{Becker2025}. \textcite{DellAcqua2023} study similarly show that \gls{ai} improves performance on some tasks while degrading it on others. Developer trust also varies: in the 2025 Stack Overflow Developer Survey, more respondents distrusted the accuracy of \gls{ai} tools (46\%) than trusted it (33\%) \parencite{StackOverflow2025}. Quality and risk concerns add further variation: \gls{ai}-generated code has been shown to contain security weaknesses \parencite{Pearce2022}, developers using \gls{ai} assistants have been found to write less secure code while believing it to be more secure \parencite{Perry2023}, and the correctness of generated code varies considerably across tasks \parencite{MoradiDakhel2023}. Taken together, these studies indicate that the value of \gls{ai} coding assistants depends on how they are used and managed, not only on the capability of the technology.

Organisational culture and leadership are recognised as determinants of whether new technologies are successfully adopted and used \parencite{Johnk2021, Uren2023}. Organisational culture refers to the shared values, norms and practices that shape how members of an organisation interpret and respond to new technology \parencite{Murire2024, An2024}. In digitally oriented organisations, a culture that supports experimentation, learning and evidence-based decision making is associated with a more positive orientation towards technology adoption \parencite{An2024, FossoWamba2024}. Recent empirical studies report that digital organisational culture strengthens the link between human \gls{ai} skills and innovation outcomes \parencite{An2024}, and that leadership, governance and employee engagement shape how \gls{ai} is embedded in organisational work \parencite{Hossain2025, Murire2024, Schneider2023}. Despite this growing recognition, research on \gls{ai} adoption remains fragmented \parencite{Romeo2025, Enholm2022}. Studies have tended either to examine \gls{ai} adoption and value at the level of the firm \parencite{Babina2024, Mikalef2021, Berente2021} or to measure effects on individual task performance \parencite{Peng2023, Noy2023, Brynjolfsson2025, Cui2026}. Comparatively few studies have integrated organisational culture, leadership and ROI in the specific context of software engineering.

African organisations have also begun adopting \gls{genai}, partly to address resource constraints and operational inefficiencies. A global survey reported that respondents in emerging economies use \gls{ai} more frequently and hold more positive views of its potential to improve productivity than respondents in advanced economies \parencite{Gillespie2025}. At the same time, the literature points to difficulties with digital skills pipelines, governance maturity and leadership capability in the adoption of \gls{genai} \parencite{Hughes2026, Isagah2024}. Without strategy, trust, and a learning orientation, the adoption of \gls{ai} is likely to be purely symbolic \parencite{Ransbotham2020}. The African setting, therefore, becomes an appropriate setting for analysing how socio-organisational factors affect the benefits that organisations derive from \gls{ai} technology.

In \gls{sa}, research has been conducted on the impact of \gls{ai} on organisations and labour. There has been research in South African organisations on the effects of the implementation of \gls{ai} on work and organisational culture  \parencite{Murire2024}, the perception of human resource managers on the effects of \gls{ai} on employees \parencite{Poisat2024}, and the effects of organizational factors like leadership and readiness on adopting new technology in the finance industry \parencite{Matsepe2022}. Further studies have proposed frameworks for \gls{ai} adoption that emphasise the interplay between people and technology \parencite{Smit2024}, and qualitative evidence from South African small and medium-sized enterprises suggests that \gls{genai} improves operational efficiency but is constrained by skills shortages, set-up costs and data-security concerns \parencite{Muzuva2024}. International reviews reach similar conclusions: the organisational benefits of \gls{ai} depend on alignment between the technology and the organisation’s culture, structure and leadership \parencite{Romeo2025, Kassa2025}. In software engineering specifically, \gls{genai} tools have been reported to improve coding productivity when they are integrated into engineering practice \parencite{Alenezi2025, Sergeyuk2025}. For software engineering teams that use \gls{ai} coding assistants, leadership and culture can therefore be expected to influence whether productivity gains are realised as productivity, quality and financial returns.

\section{Problem statement} 
\label{sec:Problem statement} 

\gls{genai} was initially used mainly for information retrieval and content generation, but its use has extended into the core processes of knowledge work. Experimental evidence shows that \gls{genai} improves the speed and quality of professional writing tasks \parencite{Noy2023}, and field evidence shows productivity improvements among customer-support agents \parencite{Brynjolfsson2025}. Studies in software development report similar effects on task completion: field experiments with GitHub Copilot found increases in completed tasks \parencite{Cui2026}, and a controlled experiment found that developers completed a programming task substantially faster with an \gls{ai} pair programmer \parencite{Peng2023}. These studies, however, measured task speed and output, not \gls{roi}. Faster completion of a task is a necessary but not a sufficient condition for a return on the organisation's investment, because the costs of licences, training, integration, review and governance, and the effects on quality and rework, determine whether time savings become organisational value \parencite{Enholm2022, Mikalef2021}.

The literature also shows that the performance effects of \gls{ai} coding assistants vary with the context in which they are used. Code generated by GitHub Copilot has been found to vary in correctness and maintainability and to require review effort \parencite{MoradiDakhel2023}, \gls{ai}  assistance has been associated with security weaknesses \parencite{Pearce2022, Perry2023}, and experienced developers working on familiar code bases have been slowed down rather than accelerated \parencite{Becker2025}. Research on \gls{ai}  governance argues that sustained value creation requires accountability structures and leadership oversight that turn experimentation into lasting performance improvement \parencite{Mantymaki2022, Schneider2023}. Studies of organisational innovation with \gls{genai} further show that performance effects differ according to whether organisations use \gls{ai}  for automation or augmentation, a choice shaped by leadership and culture \parencite{Holmstrom2025}. Research on organisational \gls{ai}  adoption adds that many organisations acquire or pilot \gls{ai} without integrating it deeply into work practices \parencite{Uren2023, Ransbotham2020}, and that top management support and organisational readiness are central to moving from experimentation to routine use \parencite{Johnk2021, Weber2023}.

These processes are particularly relevant in Africa, where most organisations face some challenges with technological adaptation, skills development and governance. \gls{ai} readiness research in Africa highlights the importance of organisational capabilty in the transition from \gls{ai} pilots to performace outcomes \parencite{Isagah2024}. In \gls{sa}, the doption of merging technologies have been shown to be influenced mostly by leadership support and organisational readiness \parencite{Matsepe2022}, and sociotechnical considerations and change management have been identified as central to \gls{ai} adoption \parencite{Smit2024}. Empirical evidence on whether, and through which mechanisms, South African software engineering teams realise returns from \gls{ai} coding assistants nevertheless remains scarce \parencite{Poisat2024, Muzuva2024}.

The problem addressed by this study was therefore as follows: although \gls{ai} coding assistants have been shown to accelerate individual programming tasks, organisations that invest in them have little empirical evidence on whether, and through which organisational mechanisms, these task-level gains translate into a return on investment. In particular, the extent to which \gls{it} leadership and organisational culture influence the \gls{roi} of \gls{ai} coding assistants, and whether they do so by improving how deeply the tools are assimilated into software engineering practice, had not been empirically examined in South African software engineering teams. Without such evidence, \gls{it} leaders cannot determine which organisational conditions to prioritise when seeking value from their investment in \gls{ai} coding assistants.

\section{Significance/rationale} 
\label{sec:Significance/rationale} 

\gls{genai} has the potential to improve efficiency in knowledge work and productivity in software engineering \parencite{Brynjolfsson2025, Peng2023, Cui2026}. However, most of the evidence on \gls{ai} coding assistants concerns task-level speed, output or developer perceptions \parencite{Peng2023, Ziegler2024, LiangJT2024, Sergeyuk2025}, while developer productivity is multidimensional and spans the individual, team and system levels \parencite{Forsgren2021}, and industry research suggests that individual gains do not automatically improve team-level delivery performance \parencite{Harvey2024}. Few studies have examined how the use of \gls{ai} coding assistants relates to the \gls{roi} that organisations realise from them.

Factors that influence whether organisations realise the benefits of \gls{ai} are leadership and organisational culture. \gls{ai}-driven capabilities are reshaping what responsible leadership requires of senior managers \parencite{Hossain2025}, and top management support and clear \gls{ai} governance have been shown to shape whether \gls{ai} moves from experimentation into routine organisational use \parencite{Johnk2021, Uren2023, DORA2025}. Cultures characterised by learning, collaboration and innovation support the development of digital capability \parencite{An2024, FossoWamba2024, Johnk2021}, and \gls{ai} capability contributes to firm performance when it is combined with organisational resources such as skills and culture \parencite{Mikalef2021}. The performance of organisations in emerging markets has also been linked to their readiness and leadership context \parencite{Gillespie2025, Isagah2024}.

Few studies, however, have examined the relationship between the productivity effects of \gls{ai} coding assistants and organisational \gls{roi}. Reviews of \gls{ai} business value note that research has concentrated on \gls{ai} capabilities and adoption, with limited attention to the organisational mechanisms through which value is realised \parencite{Enholm2022}, and research on \gls{ai} adoption in organisations remains fragmented across levels of analysis \parencite{Romeo2025}. Empirical studies that combine organisational culture, leadership, assimilation and \gls{roi} for \gls{ai} coding assistants in software engineering teams are scarce, and South African evidence on these relationships is particularly limited \parencite{Poisat2024, Smit2024, Muzuva2024}.

The study is therefore significant in three respects. Theoretically, it tested an integrated model in which assimilation quality was positioned as the mechanism through which \gls{it} leadership and organisational culture are associated with \gls{roi}, extending \gls{it} business value research \parencite{Enholm2022, Zebec2024} to \gls{ai} coding assistants. Practically, it provides \gls{it} and engineering leaders with evidence on which organisational conditions are most closely associated with the returns they obtain from \gls{ai} coding assistants. Contextually, it contributes quantitative evidence from South African software engineering teams to a literature dominated by studies from North America, Europe and Asia.

\section{Scope, Delimitations and Limitations}
\label{sec:Scope, Delimitations and Limitations} 

% ------------------------------------------------------------------------------
%  Scope
% ------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Scope}
\label{subsec:ch1-scope}
The study followed a quantitative, confirmatory research design that tested hypothesised relationships between \gls{it} leadership, organisational culture, the assimilation quality of \gls{ai} coding assistants and perceived \gls{roi}. Data were collected at a single point in time through a structured online questionnaire, and the hypothesised model was tested using \gls{pls-sem} \parencite{Hair2022, Hair2024}. The philosophical and methodological basis for these choices is set out in Chapter~3.

The study was limited to \gls{ai} coding assistants, that is, \gls{genai} tools integrated into the software development process that provide code completion, inline suggestions, code generation, debugging support and documentation. Prior studies have reported that such tools can improve task-level productivity and efficiency \parencite{Peng2023, Cui2026, Ziegler2024}.

The target population comprised software engineering practitioners, including software developers, senior developers, technical leads and engineering managers, employed by South African organisations whose teams used \gls{ai} coding assistants. Participants were recruited through non-probability sampling: the questionnaire was distributed through professional networks, \gls{it} professional societies and online technology forums, and eligibility was confirmed by a screening question on whether the respondent's team used an \gls{ai} coding assistant. The unit of analysis was the individual practitioner. Each respondent reported his or her perceptions of the leadership, culture, assimilation practices and returns associated with \gls{ai} coding assistants in his or her own team and organisation. Because responses were anonymous, it was not possible to determine how many respondents belonged to the same team or organisation, therefore the study  did not aggregate responses to the team or organisational level.

The independent variables were \gls{it} leadership and organisational culture. \gls{it} leadership was measured through executive sponsorship, metric alignment, resource support and governance clarity \parencite{Johnk2021, Schneider2023, DORA2025}. Organisational culture was measured through experimentation, knowledge sharing, learning orientation, collaboration, innovation support, responsible use and the evaluation of measurable value \parencite{An2024, Murire2024}. Assimilation quality was the mediating variable, and perceived \gls{roi} was the dependent variable, measured at the task, workflow and firm levels. Organisational readiness, team size, developer experience and adoption duration were included as control variables. These constructs are defined in Section~\ref{sec:Theoretical and conceptual framework}.

% ------------------------------------------------------------------------------
%  Delimitation
% ------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Delimitation} 
\label{subsec:ch1-delimitations}

Because the technical efficiency of \gls{ai} coding assistants has already been examined experimentally \parencite{Peng2023, Cui2026}, the study did not benchmark the accuracy of \gls{ai} tools, evaluate their algorithms or model architectures, or measure code quality directly. Instead, it focused on the socio-organisational factors associated with value realisation. The study was confined to software engineering practitioners and excluded other professional groups that use \gls{genai}, such as marketing, finance and legal teams, because software engineering workflows differ substantially from those of other professions.

\gls{it} leadership and organisational culture were examined as the main independent variables. Other factors that may influence \gls{roi} such as national regulation, macroeconomic conditions and vendor pricing models, were not examined, because the study focused on factors internal to the organisation. \gls{roi} was measured through practitioners' perceptions of realised value rather than through financial data, because individual practitioners do not typically have access to the cost and revenue records that a financial \gls{roi} calculation requires. Perceptual measures of \gls{ai} business value and organisational performance are an established approach in recent \gls{is} research when objective data are unavailable to respondents \parencite{Mikalef2021, FossoWamba2024, AbdulWahab2024}.

% ------------------------------------------------------------------------------
%  Brief Limitations
% ------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Limitations}
\label{subsec:ch1-limitations}

The study had several limitations. First, \gls{roi} was measured through respondents'
 perceptions of productivity, quality and business value rather than through financial records, and perceived \gls{roi} may therefore differ from the financial return realised by organisations. Second, each respondent completed the questionnaire once and in that single questionnaire, answered the items for all constructs, including both the predictors (\gls{it} leadership, organisational culture and assimilation quality) and the outcome (perceived \gls{roi}). Because the predictors and the outcome came from the same person, using the same response format on the same occasion, the relationships between them may be partly inflated by the shared measurement method rather than reflecting only true relationships. This is known as common method bias  \parencite{Podsakoff2024}.. Third, the design was cross-sectional; it established associations between constructs at one point in time but cannot establish causality, and it could not capture effects of culture and leadership that may take longer to become visible in financial results \parencite{Brynjolfsson2021}. Fourth, non-probability sampling through professional networks limits the extent to which the findings can be generalised to all South African software engineering practitioners. Finally, because responses were anonymous, the study could not verify organisational characteristics such as organisation size, nor account for multiple respondents from the same team or organisation. These limitations are discussed further in Chapters~3, 5 and~6.

\section{Theoretical and conceptual framework}
\label{sec:ch1-framework}

This section distinguishes between the theoretical framework of the study, which consists of the established theories that explain why the constructs were expected to be related, and the conceptual framework, which is the study's own model of the constructs and the hypothesised relationships between them.

\subsection{Theoretical framework}
\label{subsec:ch1-theoretical}

The study was grounded in two complementary theories: \gls{it} value realisation theory and socio-technical systems theory.

\paragraph{\gls{it} value realisation theory.} \gls{it} value realisation theory originated in information systems research on \gls{it} business value, which sought to explain why investment in technology does not consistently produce performance gains. \textcite{Soh1995} synthesised this research into a process theory in which \gls{it} expenditure is converted into \gls{it} assets, \gls{it} assets produce \gls{it} impacts only when they are used appropriately, and \gls{it} impacts are translated into organisational performance. \textcite{Melville2004} extended the theory in an integrative model of \gls{it} business value, in which \gls{it} resources create value within business processes and in combination with complementary organisational resources. Value can be lost at each stage, and the theory therefore emphasises the complementary organisational resources, such as skills, work practices, leadership and culture, that determine whether technology is used productively \parencite{Mikalef2021, Enholm2022}. Recent research has applied this logic to \gls{ai}. \textcite{Enholm2022} describe how organisational enablers and inhibitors shape the first-order, process-level effects of \gls{ai} and, through them, its second-order effects on firm performance; \textcite{Zebec2024} adapted the integrative \gls{it} business value model to explain how \gls{ai} creates value through business process capabilities; and economic research shows that the returns from \gls{ai} depend on complementary intangible investment \parencite{Brynjolfsson2021}.

\gls{it} value realisation theory contributed three elements to the study's model. First, it justified distinguishing between the acquisition of \gls{ai} coding assistants and their productive use, which the study captured through the construct of assimilation quality \parencite{AbdulWahab2024, Uren2023}. Second, it identified organisational complements, in this study \gls{it} leadership and organisational culture, as the conditions under which technology is used productively \parencite{Mikalef2021, Enholm2022}. Third, it located organisational value at the end of the sequence, which the study captured through perceived \gls{roi} at the task, workflow and firm levels \parencite{Enholm2022, Zebec2024}. Together, these elements provided the theoretical basis for positioning assimilation quality as the mediator between the organisational antecedents and \gls{roi}.

\paragraph{Socio-technical systems theory.} \gls{sts} theory originated in the Tavistock Institute's studies of work organisation in British coal mining, which found that new mechanised methods did not deliver the expected productivity when the social organisation of work was not redesigned together with the technology \parencite{Trist1951}. Recent \gls{is} research continues to build on this perspective \parencite{Bednar2020}. The theory holds that an organisation consists of interdependent social and technical subsystems, and that performance depends on the joint optimisation of both rather than on the optimisation of the technology alone \parencite{Bednar2020, Makarius2020}. In \gls{is} research, the sociotechnical perspective has been used to design systems that are both effective and meaningful for the people who use them \parencite{Bednar2020}. \textcite{Makarius2020} extended the perspective to AI, arguing that organisations realise value from AI only when they develop the human capabilities, roles and structures that allow employees to work alongside it, and South African research has proposed an \gls{ai} adoption framework built on the interaction between people and \gls{ai} \parencite{Smit2024}.

\gls{sts} theory contributed to the study's model by explaining why the value of \gls{ai} coding assistants depends on the social subsystem in which they are used. \gls{ai} coding assistants form part of the technical subsystem, while \gls{it} leadership and organisational culture shape the social subsystem: the norms, roles, governance arrangements and learning practices of software engineering teams. From this perspective, \gls{roi} is expected to depend on how well the technology is integrated into the team's work practices, that is, on assimilation quality, rather than on the technology alone \parencite{Bednar2020, Makarius2020, Smit2024}.

The two theories are complementary. \gls{it} value realisation theory specifies the sequence through which \gls{it} investment becomes value (acquisition, use, impact), while \gls{sts} theory explains why the social conditions created by leadership and culture determine the quality of the use stage.

\subsection{Conceptual framework}
\label{subsec:ch1-conceptual}

Drawing on these theories, the study proposed the conceptual model presented in Figure~\ref{fig:ch1-conceptual-model}. The model positions \gls{it} leadership and organisational culture as antecedents, assimilation quality as the mediator, and perceived \gls{roi} as the outcome. The constructs were defined as follows.

\begin{description}
	\item[\gls{it} leadership] The behaviours through which \gls{it} and engineering leaders sponsor, resource, govern and set measurable expectations for the use of \gls{ai} coding assistants. It comprises executive sponsorship, metric alignment, resource support and governance clarity \parencite{Johnk2021, Schneider2023, DORA2025}.
	\item[Organisational culture] The shared norms and practices that shape how software engineering teams approach \gls{ai} coding assistants, captured through experimentation, knowledge sharing, learning orientation, collaboration, innovation support, responsible use and the evaluation of measurable value \parencite{Murire2024, EdmondsonBransby2023, An2024}.
	\item[Assimilation quality] The extent to which \gls{ai}coding assistants are integrated productively into software engineering work rather than merely installed, captured through workflow integration, usage consistency, trust calibration (critical review of \gls{ai}-generated output) and training and governance support \parencite{AbdulWahab2024, Weber2023, Sergeyuk2025}.
	\item[Perceived \gls{roi}] Practitioners' assessment of the value realised from \gls{ai} coding assistants relative to the investment made, at three levels: the task level (individual productivity and quality), the workflow level (team efficiency, rework and delivery) and the firm level (development cost, time to market, innovation and financial value) \parencite{Enholm2022, Mikalef2021}.
	\item[Control variables] Organisational readiness (infrastructure, skills, governance, risk preparedness and the capability to assess value), team size, developer experience and adoption duration, which were included because they may influence perceived \gls{roi} independently of the hypothesised relationships \parencite{Isagah2024, Cui2026}.
\end{description}

The model specified three sets of relationships. First, organisational culture (H5) and \gls{it} leadership (H6) were expected to be positively associated with assimilation quality, because supportive norms and active leadership enable teams to integrate, use and govern \gls{ai} coding assistants effectively \parencite{Johnk2021, Uren2023, An2024}. Second, assimilation quality was expected to be positively associated with perceived \gls{roi} (H3), because value arises from the productive use of a technology rather than from its acquisition \parencite{Zebec2024, AbdulWahab2024}. Third, organisational culture (H1) and \gls{it} leadership (H2) were expected to be positively associated with perceived \gls{roi}, and assimilation quality was expected to mediate these associations (H4a and H4b). Assimilation quality is therefore the mediator in the model; \gls{it} leadership and organisational culture are the antecedent variables whose associations with perceived \gls{roi} were expected to operate, wholly or partly, through assimilation quality. The control variables were specified with direct paths to perceived \gls{roi} only. The hypotheses are developed in full in Chapter~2.

\section{Chapter summary}
\label{sec:ch1-summary}

This chapter introduced a study of the role of \gls{it} leadership and organisational culture in realising the \gls{roi} of \gls{ai} coding assistants in South African software engineering teams. It presented the background to the study, which showed that the task-level productivity gains reported for \gls{ai} coding assistants do not necessarily translate into organisational value, and stated the research problem explicitly. The chapter then set out the research aim, five research objectives and five research questions, and showed in Table~\ref{tab:ch1-alignment} how each research question was answered by a hypothesis or an analysis. It explained the significance of the study, defined its scope, delimitations and limitations, and presented the theoretical framework, consisting of \gls{it} value realisation theory and socio-technical systems theory, together with the conceptual model of the study. The next chapter reviews the literature on which the study's hypotheses are based.
